# Understanding Yourself: First See How You Operate

When people say, “I do not understand myself,” the missing piece is rarely another personality test. More often, they keep issuing verdicts about themselves: I am a procrastinator; I am not smart enough; I have no language talent; I always fail when I get nervous.

A verdict can look like knowledge while being nothing more than an unexamined wound. You turn one difficult afternoon into a personality, another person’s sentence into a destiny, and then use that sentence to explain every later retreat. It is efficient, but it leaves no room to change the conditions.

Understanding yourself does not begin by answering “Who am I, once and for all?” It begins by seeing how you operate: what triggers you, what action follows, what result that action produces, and what you were trying to protect. Once those patterns are visible, “That is just who I am” can become “Under these conditions, I usually respond this way; I can change one condition first.”

## What This Chapter Covers

- Separate facts, interpretations, judgments, and wishes;
- Observe recurring patterns through trigger, action, result, and need;
- Recognize your capacity, attention, and avoidance triggers;
- Treat friction in English learning as feedback rather than a verdict on your character;
- Use seven days of observation instead of one permanent label.

## 1. Stop Issuing Verdicts About Yourself

“I am not disciplined” is not complete information. It may mean the task is too large, the starting point is too far away, your capacity was low that day, or this is not the problem you truly need to solve right now.

Split a self-judgment into four lines:

| Record | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Observable fact | I did not make a speaking recording for three days; each time I opened the file, I checked messages first. |
| My interpretation | I may be afraid of hearing myself make mistakes. |
| Emotion and body | My chest tightens before recording; I want to leave when I imagine someone judging me. |
| What is still unknown | Would I still avoid it if the recording were private? |

Facts can be checked by someone else. Interpretations need evidence. Emotions are real experiences, but they are not always conclusions. Separating them does not instantly make you stronger; it gives you a workable starting point.

## 2. Observe Recurring Patterns with Four Questions

A response that appears three times deserves one written observation. Ask four questions:

1. **What happened?** Write the time, setting, and concrete action. Do not write “I was terrible.”
2. **What did I do?** Did you leave, delay, explain, appease, overcommit, or ask for help?
3. **What did it produce?** What relief did you get immediately, and what cost arrived later?
4. **What was I protecting?** Face, time, relationship, safety, control, or an expired goal?

Suppose you promise to start English tomorrow but spend two hours organising materials instead. The immediate benefit is feeling as if you have begun; the cost is that you produced nothing. What may be protected is the image of being serious, rather than actual communication ability. That observation is not an insult. It suggests that next time you should complete five minutes of checkable output before organising anything.

## 3. See Your Capacity Instead of Worshipping Your Ideal State

The same person does not have the same attention after good sleep, repeated interruptions, caring for family, or a week of late work. A plan that describes only “what I should do” will turn capacity differences into moral differences.

For one week, record three variables before each learning or work session:

| Variable | Low-capacity signals | Possible adjustment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Body | Poor sleep, pain, hunger, rapid heartbeat | Lower the difficulty; meet basic needs first. |
| Attention | Constant switching; unable to finish a paragraph | Remove one source of distraction and focus on one task. |
| Emotion | Fear of judgment, irritation, numbness | Make a private version first, or tell a trusted person how you are doing. |

This is not a search for a biological excuse for every problem. It turns “Why can I not do this today?” into a condition you can adjust. A low-capacity day can still keep one small action: repeat three sentences, listen to a short passage and write its main point, or organise one error. If even that is unsafe or unrealistic, recovery is the task for that day.

## 4. Treat Avoidance as Information, Not Identity

Avoidance usually protects something. Rewriting your plan again and again may protect you from the uncertainty of starting. Choosing only easy material may protect you from exposing your real level. Hiding a project may protect you from an evaluation you cannot control.

Record a recent avoidance:

```markdown
What I intended to do:
What I actually did:
Relief I gained immediately:
Cost I postponed:
If I made the task 80% smaller, what would I be willing to do first:
```

Making something smaller is not lowering the standard forever. It lets information return. A three-sentence draft can reveal whether the real difficulty is the idea, vocabulary, time, or fear of delivery. Without a draft, every difficulty remains mixed together.

## 5. English Learning Is a Concrete Mirror

English can magnify patterns that are hard to notice elsewhere: you speak smoothly on familiar topics but go silent under an unfamiliar question; you recognise words but cannot retrieve them in a sentence; you follow a lesson but miss a colleague’s spontaneous explanation. None of these differences can be explained by “talent” alone.

Classify each block as one of four types:

| Type of block | Possible underlying problem | Change one condition next time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I do not know what to say | No viewpoint, example, or structure | Write three points in your first language, then turn them into English. |
| I know the meaning but cannot say it | Retrieval is too slow | Record a 60-second repetition on the same situation. |
| I hear sounds but cannot follow | Missing links, emphasis, or context | Read the task question first, then listen to a short segment closely. |
| I tense up as soon as I speak | The expected judgment is too heavy | Record a private version before choosing a safe listener. |

The value of a learning log is not proving that you worked hard. It is separating the problem so the practice does not have to rely on willpower alone.

## 6. Seven-Day Self-Observation Practice

For one week, do not rush to change yourself. Collect seven days of samples, no more than five minutes per day:

- **Day 1:** Record a moment when you issued a verdict about yourself. Separate fact from interpretation.
- **Day 2:** Record one body signal that appeared before starting.
- **Day 3:** Record one avoidance, including its short-term benefit and long-term cost.
- **Day 4:** Complete a low-capacity English output.
- **Day 5:** Classify one block and write the one condition you will change next time.
- **Day 6:** Ask a trusted person to describe what you do under pressure. Do not ask for a personality judgment.
- **Day 7:** Circle triggers and actions that appeared at least twice. Choose one for next week’s experiment.

Copy this record:

```markdown
Date and setting:
Observable facts:
The action I took:
Immediate relief:
Later cost:
What I may have been protecting:
The one condition I will change next time:
```

## 7. Make One Small Decision After Observing

Self-observation can become another form of postponement if it only produces notes. At the end of seven days, choose one common and adjustable pattern. Turn it into a condition:

> When I notice [signal] in the [setting], I will first do [action], then decide whether to continue.

For example: when I keep revising before a meeting because I am afraid of making a mistake, I will first record a private 60-second explanation with one fact and one risk, then decide whether more editing is needed. This does not guarantee a perfect performance. It lets action come before judgment.

If a pattern involves persistent insomnia, intense fear, thoughts of self-harm, or a marked loss of daily function, take the record to a qualified local medical or mental-health professional. Self-observation can support a conversation; it cannot replace professional assessment.

## Conclusion: Become a Reliable Observer of Yourself

Knowing yourself is not finding a beautiful definition. It is being willing to revise an old conclusion when the facts no longer support it. You can admit fear and still ask: What is the fear protecting? What support is needed now? How small can the next step be?

English learning, project delivery, and relationship conflict will keep bringing these questions back. The more clearly you see how you operate, the less you need shame to push you, and the more likely you are to choose an action that fits your actual life.

Do not hurry to become the ideal person. Today, become someone who can see what is happening inside their own life. That is already ground for the next change.
